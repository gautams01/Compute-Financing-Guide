Financing data centers & GPUs

Borrower

Sources of capitalWhere the money comes from

Equity

Venture capital & strategic equity
Equity from VCs, growth funds and strategic investors such as chip vendors and hyperscalers. The most expensive and dilutive capital, but it provides the equity cushion lenders require: venture debt and asset-backed loans are sized against it. funding need
Internal cash flow
Fund capex from operating cash flow. Hyperscalers fund most of their buildout this way. For a startup, margin from existing clusters funds the next one: no dilution or leverage, but slower growth. funding need

GPU Debt

Vendor financing
Deferred payment terms (60–180 days) or 3–5yr loans and leases from OEM finance arms. Easiest debt to get: vendors lend on their sale margin and can recover and resell the gear. Limits: down payment or guarantee, cost may be buried in a smaller discount, first lien on the gear (needs venture-debt carve-out), vendor lock-in. residual value
Equipment leasing
Equipment leaseA lessor owns the GPUs and leases them to you; operating lease or finance lease with a purchase option. Sale-leasebacks work the same way for GPUs you already own. Lessor retains residual risk.
Ring-fenced lease (SPV)At scale, a special purpose vehicle raises equity and debt, buys the GPUs and leases them to the user (e.g. 5yr). Lenders’ claim is limited to the chips and lease payments, separate from the user’s other creditors. Outside investors, including chip vendors, can invest in the SPV equity.
residual value, user credit
Venture debt
Term loans from venture lenders and bank tech desks, underwritten on equity backing rather than assets. Usually comes with warrants. Revenue-based financing is a variant for companies with recurring revenue.
TermsSized at ~20–35% of the last equity round; 3–4yr, interest-only then amortizing; fees plus warrants; blanket lien and minimum-cash covenant.
Repaid from equity, not GPUsA cluster's cash flow typically covers only part of the debt service; the rest comes from the equity raise. A bridge until offtake contracts support GPU-backed debt.
Negotiate a carve-outThe blanket lien blocks later GPU loans and leases unless future equipment financing is excluded.
funding need
GPU-backed term loans
Secured by GPUs plus assigned offtake contract. Delayed draw tied to chip delivery; amortizes over the contract term.
Contract is the real collateralOfftaker consent to assignment, no early termination for convenience or SLA misses, tenor at least matching the loan.
Outpace depreciationFront-loaded amortization, LTV tests with cash sweep, small or insured balloon.
EnforcementColo access agreement, backup operator, fire-sale risk if many lenders sell the same GPU model.
PricingOfftaker credit spread plus operator execution premium; commitment fees on undrawn amounts.
offtaker credit, residual value
Mezzanine & preferred equity
Subordinated capital that fills the gap between senior debt (70–80% LTV) and sponsor equity. Can sit at the project, SPV or parent level; often PIK. Preferred equity ranks below all debt. Unlike corporate debt, available against a single project. funding need
Corporate debt
Senior debt at the parent, repaid from total company cash flow rather than one asset; can be unsecured or secured by all assets. Generally needs scale and operating history.
FormsIG bonds (hyperscalers), HY bonds, leveraged loans, converts (popular with volatile public stocks), revolvers.
Structural subordinationParent bondholders are paid only after asset-level SPV lenders, so they price wider even when both are “senior.”
CovenantsHY baskets cap how much secured and SPV debt can be layered ahead of the bonds.

Warehouse to ABS
Fill a revolving warehouse line (65–75% advance, floating, 2–3yr) with eligible contracts. Once the pool is large and seasoned, issue tranched ABS and use the proceeds to repay the warehouse, then refill it.
Why term outLower blended cost from AAA–A senior notes; higher advance rate, so less equity per dollar of assets; fixed-rate, longer-dated debt that amortizes with the contracts; warehouse capacity recycled for new deals; access to insurers and ABS funds.
Why not GPUs yetShort chip life caps term and advance rate; little resale data to support residual value; pools concentrated in a few offtakers cap ratings. Could work as GPU contracts lengthen, price indices develop and offtakers diversify.
concentration, refinancing
SPV project bonds
A sponsor puts one campus in a ring-fenced SPV, signs a long lease with a single tenant, and the SPV issues bonds to fund construction. Bondholders are repaid from the lease, secured by the land, building and assigned lease. Sponsor equity is the only credit support.
Investment gradeIG hyperscaler tenant, 15–20yr triple-net lease. Fully amortizing, ~90–97% debt financed. Rating and spread track the tenant’s.
High yieldLower-rated or backstopped tenant. 5NC2, partial amortization, refi at year 5; typically ~80% debt financed.
Construction protectionsFixed-price construction contract, completion guarantee from the sponsor, capitalized interest reserve until rent starts, delay damages from the contractor.
What to underwriteTenant credit and concentration; lease termination rights, especially for late delivery; whether rent starts on delivery regardless of tenant fit-out; refi risk on HY bullets.
Who can use itDevelopers with a signed lease from a highly rated tenant. A startup is usually the tenant, not the issuer.
construction, tenant credit

Credit enhancementsHow a borrower becomes bankable

Debt structure

Sizing & pricing
GPU loans at 70–80% LTV. Sized to at least 1.3x DSCR, tested assuming any backstop is triggered. Priced at offtaker credit spread plus an execution premium.
Debt terms
InterestFixed or floating; floating usually requires swaps on most of the balance. PIK toggle during ramp; margin step-downs at utilization milestones.
AmortizationSculpted to contracted cash flow, minimal balloon. Cash sweep below a utilization threshold.
MaturityInside the lease or offtake term. For GPUs, also inside the chip's useful life; sub-1yr rental books are hard to finance.
PrepaymentNon-call periods, make-wholes and prepayment fees set how cheaply you can refinance later.
residual value, re-leasing
Recourse
Non-recourse (SPV assets only), limited (parent guarantees completion or overruns) or full (parent guarantee). Startups with thin contracts often have to give some. offtaker credit, construction
Collateral & covenants
Bankruptcy-remote SPV per asset or contract with step-in rights; lockbox and DSCR covenant.
ReservesDebt service reserve (~6 months), maintenance reserve for failed GPUs, interest reserve during construction.
Cash trapsBelow a DSCR lock-up level, equity distributions stop; lower still, a cash sweep pays down debt.
offtaker credit
Borrowing base
Availability recalculated periodically from eligible contracts and appraised GPU values; shrinks automatically as contracts roll off or chips lose value. residual value
Intercreditor terms
Sets payment priority and enforcement rights when venture debt, mezzanine, GPU loans and vendor liens coexist; where equipment carve-outs live.
Equity kicker
Warrants to the lender in exchange for lower coupon or higher advance rate.

Cash flow enhancement

Customer prepayments
Up to 100% of contract value upfront on 1yr compute deals. Reduces the amount borrowed and the equity required. offtaker credit, funding need
Customer-funded hardware
Customer buys the GPUs and the operator hosts them, or the tenant funds the fit-out. Shrinks what the operator has to finance. funding need, residual value
Revenue share
Lender receives a share of GPU-hour revenue in exchange for a lower base coupon. utilization, price
Multiple offtakers
Single GPU contract supported by two offtakers with complementary workloads (daytime inference, nighttime training). Higher utilization, diversified payment risk. offtaker credit, utilization
Contract terms
Take-or-pay or minimum commitments, tenor matching the debt, no termination for convenience, price escalators, power cost pass-through. A reserved base plus on-demand upside works too; lenders size off the base. offtaker credit, utilization, power price
Resale rights
Contract rights to resell a customer’s unused reserved capacity, which also makes customers more willing to sign longer take-or-pay commitments. Uncontracted GPUs go to spot marketplaces or brokers. utilization

Credit support

3rd party credit backstop
NVIDIA, AMD or Google guarantee of lease payments, or take-or-pay floor on GPU capacity (typically ~6yr, declining price curve). The lender underwrites the backstopper's credit. Covers the full contract term, so it substitutes for the customer's credit.
Backstopper compensationShare of revenue above the floor, warrants or equity stake, or a fee.
Related-party exposureLenders haircut credit when the backstopper is also the supplier and an equity investor.
offtaker credit, utilization
Customer credit support
Letter of credit from the customer’s bank, security deposit or parent guarantee from weaker customers such as startup labs. LCs and deposits usually cover only a few months of payments: a cushion while the GPUs are re-leased, not a replacement for the customer’s credit. offtaker credit

Futures & options
Hedge GPU-hour pricing on uncontracted capacity; rate and power hedges alongside. No liquid GPU derivatives market or widely used price index yet. price, residual value
Power purchase agreements
10–20yr contract to buy power at a set price. Fixes the largest operating cost, stabilizing DSCR.
TypesPhysical (power delivered), virtual (financial hedge settled against a hub price), sleeved (utility delivers and passes the price through).

Rent from cloud

Reserve GPU capacity from a neocloud or hyperscaler on a take-or-pay contract, then resell it: part under fixed-price customer contracts, the rest on-demand at market prices that rise and then fall. Compared with buying on-demand capacity only when you need it. Modeled monthly, shown by year.

Model notes and assumptions
  • Monthly model. Cash flows are calculated month by month and summed into years for the table. IRR is annualized from the monthly IRR; NPV discounts monthly at the hurdle rate.
  • Take-or-pay: every reserved hour is billed whether or not you sell it. The prepayment is paid at signing at a discount; the rest is billed monthly, quarterly or annually, in advance or in arrears, starting at delivery. A security deposit (months of billing) is paid at signing and returned at the end.
  • Delivery and SLA: capacity can arrive late while the prepayment is already paid. Availability below 100% reduces hours you can sell; the SLA credit refunds that share of the fee on missed hours.
  • Two sales channels: a share of delivered hours is sold to contract customers at a fixed price on take-or-pay terms. Customer contracts start at delivery and last the customer contract length; once they end, those hours are sold on-demand at market price and on-demand utilization. A customer contract longer than your own term is cut off at your term. Contract customers can prepay a share of their contract at signing, which offsets your own prepayment; the rest is billed monthly. Cash flow margin and other costs use revenue as earned, not as billed. The rest of delivered hours are sold on-demand at the market price plus markup, with utilization ramping from a starting level to steady state.
  • Market price path: one market on-demand price drives both sides: you resell on-demand hours at that price plus your markup, and the alternative buys hours at that price. That price rises at one rate until the peak (years from today), then changes at another rate. Contract and reserved prices are fixed.
  • On-demand alternative: buys on-demand capacity only for the hours you sell, at the market rate, but only a share of needed hours can be obtained; revenue on hours you can’t get is lost.
  • No residual value, taxes or working capital. Defaults are illustrative starting points, not market quotes. Change them to match a real deal.

Colo + own GPUs

Returns on one GPU: short-life compute capex, revenue from contract and on-demand sales on a market price path, debt that amortizes over the GPU’s life, and downtime that reduces billable hours. Colo rent, power and ops are operating costs; long-life facility costs sit in the colo rent rather than being depreciated over the GPU life.

Model notes and base-case rationale
  • Capex is short-life compute only: $50k GPU/platform plus $15k networking and deployment kit. Powered shell and other long-life facility costs stay in direct opex to avoid an asset-life mismatch and double counting.
  • Sales and market price: a share of available hours is sold to contract customers at a fixed price for the customer contract length, starting at first revenue; after that, those hours are sold on-demand. On-demand hours sell at the market on-demand price plus a markup, with utilization ramping to steady state. The market price rises until the peak (years from today), then changes at another rate. Customers can prepay a share of their contract at signing (t0, when the GPUs are bought); that revenue is earned over the contract but its cash arrives upfront.
  • Operating costs are split into colo rent, power and ops. Colo rent of $200/kW-month on 1.4 kW of IT load per GPU, escalating 3% a year, sits within mid-2026 primary-market asking rents of roughly $190–235. It is fixed: it runs from powered-shell handoff whether or not the GPUs are billing. Power at $80/MWh × IT load × PUE 1.3 assumes pass-through at about the US industrial rate, and is billed only on hours the GPUs are running. Ops & other at $4,000/GPU/yr covers network, remote hands, support and insurance for a deployment of a few hundred GPUs; large operators run closer to $1,500–3,000, small teams with their own staff well above.
  • Financing: 75% loan-to-cost at 7% over 5 years, amortizing with equal principal; the same GPU debt terms as the modular tab and the powered shell’s investment-grade case. Interest during the deployment delay is funded by equity at close.
  • Colo outages: facility uptime of 99.95% (about 4 hours a year) is applied on top of your own availability, so outages cut billable hours and power. The colo SLA credits a multiple of the rent for the hours down (5x by default), capped at a set number of months of rent per year. Rent is under $0.40 per GPU-hour against over $4 of revenue, so credits rarely make up for lost sales; credits you owe your own customers and hardware damage are not modeled.
  • Colo security deposit of 3 months’ rent is paid at signing and returned when the lease ends.
  • Deployment delay of 1.5 months follows a 1–2 month install window after powered-shell handoff. It defers revenue rather than losing it: the GPUs still earn for their full life, so the stub runs past year five, while the market price path keeps moving.
  • Residual value is zero; any resale is upside. No taxes are modeled.

Modular data center

Buy prefabricated power and cooling modules plus GPUs, and site them where power is cheap or available quickly. Faster to revenue than a building, but the startup owns more of the stack and takes site and power risk.

Model notes and assumptions

  • Sales and market price: a share of available hours is sold to contract customers at a fixed price for the customer contract length, starting at first revenue; after that, those hours are sold on-demand.   • Timeline: module capex is paid at t0; GPUs are bought and revenue starts after the months-to-revenue delay, then runs for the GPU life.
  • Defaults: modules at $5,500/kW of IT load, a little above recent small modular AI contracts of about $4.4–4.6M per MW, which exclude site work and the utility connection; site prep and hookup add $1,400/kW of IT load (about $1.4M per MW), from under $1M on a site with an existing substation to $2M+ on greenfield. Power at $55/MWh assumes on-site gas or stranded power: fuel at $3–4/MMBtu plus generator capital and O&M. Ops & other of $4,000/GPU/yr matches the colo tab (network, support, insurance); site and module O&M adds $1,000 for maintenance, on-site staff and a ground lease. GPU capex matches colo: $50k GPU plus $15k networking and deployment kit.
  • Modules outlive the GPUs. At exit, the module book value (straight-line over module life) times the recovery percentage comes back as exit value. The default 50% of book (about 25% of original cost after 5.5 years) allows for denser next-generation GPUs, relocation costs, site work that can’t be moved, and a thin resale market; a buyback or a planned GPU refresh in the same modules would justify more. Set GPU generations to 2 to model a refresh inside the same modules (12-year module life); the default is 1.
  • Financing: GPU debt amortizes over the GPU life from revenue start; module financing amortizes over its term from revenue start, with any balance repaid at exit. Interest before revenue is paid by equity.
  • Power is billed on total facility load (IT load × PUE) at full load during operating months. No taxes. Defaults are illustrative starting points, not market quotes. Change them to match a real deal.

Own powered shell

Build or buy the building, power and cooling, then fill it with GPUs. The longest wait for revenue and the most capital, but the lowest operating cost and a long-lived asset that outlasts several GPU generations.

Model notes and assumptions
  • Per GPU: facility capex is entered per kW of IT load (land, shell, power and cooling) and converted using IT load per GPU.
  • Sales and market price: a share of available hours is sold to contract customers at a fixed price for the customer contract length, starting at first revenue; after that, those hours are sold on-demand. On-demand hours sell at the market on-demand price plus a markup, with utilization ramping to steady state. The market price rises until the peak (years from today), then changes at another rate. Customers can prepay a share of their contract when the GPUs are bought, typically to help fund them; that revenue is earned over the contract but its cash arrives upfront.
  • Timeline: facility capex is spent evenly over the construction months, drawn pro rata from equity and facility debt. GPUs are bought when the facility is ready and replaced at the end of each useful life, for the number of GPU generations set (4 × 5 years by default, a 22-year horizon). Each new generation re-prices at today’s market and runs the same sales assumptions: new contracts, the same ramp and the same rise-then-fall price path from its own start.
  • Defaults: facility capex of $13,000/kW of IT load ($13M per MW) sits above JLL’s 2026 global average of $11.3M per MW, since liquid cooling and AI-ready design add $1–2M per MW; Cushman & Wakefield puts US all-in greenfield builds at $8.9–23.3M per MW. Power at $75/MWh assumes a grid tariff or PPA slightly below the US industrial average. Ops & other of $4,000/GPU/yr matches the colo tab (network, support, insurance); facility O&M adds $500 for maintenance and property tax, with no rent. GPU capex matches colo: $50k GPU plus $15k networking and deployment kit.
  • Facility exit value: straight-line book value at the end of the horizon times the recovery percentage, standing in for a sale, sale-leaseback or re-tenanting. With four generations the building is 20 years into a 25-year life at exit.
  • Real terms: GPU capex, opex and contract prices are held flat across generations, so later generations assume newer GPUs cost and earn about what today’s do.
  • Financing: facility debt is drawn alongside construction spend and carries one rate from financial close to maturity; interest during construction is capitalized into the balance. From completion it amortizes evenly until maturity (years from financial close), so a set share is repaid by then; any remaining balance is refinanced and fully amortized over the refinancing term, or repaid at maturity if that term is 0. Any balance left at exit is repaid then. Each GPU generation is financed separately: drawn at purchase and amortized over the GPU debt term.
  • Offtaker credit sets the debt terms, which can then be edited. Investment grade (e.g. a hyperscaler lease): 90/10 debt to equity on the facility at 6.5%, a 15-year fully amortizing term; GPU debt at 75% and 7% over 5 years. High yield (e.g. a neocloud or startup): 80/20 debt to equity at 10%, a 5-year term with 25% amortized by maturity, then the remaining 75% refinanced into investment-grade terms (6.5%, fully amortizing over 10 years) once the building is complete and leased; GPU debt at 60% and 11% over 4 years. Both facility structures run 15 years in total. Rates are illustrative, not quotes.

Reading the comparison
  • Horizons differ. Rent from cloud runs for its commitment term, colo and modular for one GPU life, and the powered shell for several GPU generations in the same building. A longer horizon earns a bigger NPV, so compare equivalent annual NPV (NPV spread over the horizon as an annuity at the hurdle rate) and IRR, not raw NPV. The hurdle rate from the colo tab is used for all four.
  • Rent from cloud carries no debt, so its project and equity IRR are the same return on upfront cash, and leverage rows don’t apply.
  • Shared assumptions (sales mix, contract price and length, customer prepayment, market price path, GPU capex and GPU debt terms) start from the same defaults on every tab, so differences come from the structure. They only stay aligned if you change them on every tab.
  • What differs on purpose: time to first revenue, availability (a cloud provider’s SLA versus running your own hardware), facility cost and power price, and facility financing.

Tax & public incentives

Federal, state and public financing levers that lower the cost of GPUs, facilities and power. Status as of October 2026; state programs are changing quickly.

Federal tax

100% bonus depreciation (§168(k))
How it helpsImmediately expense GPUs, servers, electrical and cooling. With no taxable income, get the value through a lessor or tax-equity partner.
Watch forProperty placed in service after Jan 19, 2025; now permanent. CA, MA and NY don’t conform.
§179 expensing
How it helpsAlternative immediate expensing for smaller purchases.
Watch forCapped at $2.5M, phasing out above $5M of purchases; immaterial at cluster scale.
R&D credit (§41)
How it helpsPayments to use someone else’s computers for qualified research count toward the credit, so cloud GPU spend on research can qualify. Startups can apply up to $500K/yr against payroll tax.
Watch forHardware must be owned and run by others, off-site, and not primarily used by you; allocate mixed use; keep usage records. Payroll offset needs under $5M of receipts and no receipts more than 5 years back.
R&D expensing (§174)
How it helpsDomestic R&D deductible immediately again from 2025.
Watch forForeign R&D still amortized over 15 years.
Losses (NOLs & §382)
How it helpsLosses from depreciation carry forward to offset future profits.
Watch forCan offset only 80% of taxable income per year. A >50% ownership shift over 3 years, common after big VC rounds, caps annual use.
Interest deduction (§163(j))
How it helpsCap is 30% of adjusted taxable income, now measured before depreciation; leveraged builds deduct more interest.
Watch forTax years after 2024; some states decouple.
Energy credits (§48E / §45Y)
How it helpsCredits on on-site storage, fuel cells and generation.
Watch forStorage, fuel cells, geothermal: full credit for construction starting through 2033, 75% in 2034, 50% in 2035. Solar and wind: construction by July 4, 2026, else in service by end-2027. Foreign-entity sourcing limits.
Credit transfer (§6418)
How it helpsSell credits for cash, typically at a discount; lenders bridge against expected sales.
Watch forNo transfers to specified foreign entities.

State & local

Equipment sales tax exemptions
How it helpsExempts servers and equipment from sales tax, often the largest state tax on a GPU build. Colo tenants may qualify through the operator.
Watch forInvestment and job thresholds (Illinois: $250M over 5yrs, 20 jobs). 2026: Ohio and Illinois paused new approvals, Washington narrowed its program, Virginia’s is under budget review.
Power tax exemptions & utility rates
How it helpsSome states exempt data center electricity from sales tax; utilities offer economic development rates for large new loads.
Watch forVaries by state and utility; new large-load tariffs often add minimum bills.
Property tax abatements
How it helpsLocal PILOT agreements, often through industrial revenue bonds: the city holds title and leases the property back, so it’s tax-exempt.
Watch forInvestment deadlines and claw-backs; political risk (Independence, MO granted Nebius a 90% abatement in March 2026; a supporting councilman was recalled in September).

Investment & public financing

Opportunity zones
How it helpsInvestors defer and reduce capital gains tax by investing in projects in designated zones; helps raise equity for a site there.
Watch forMade permanent; new rolling 10-year zone designations start in 2027.
Export credit agencies
How it helpsGovernment-backed loans for US equipment used at non-US sites; longer tenor, lower cost.
Watch forUS content requirements.
Federal energy loans
How it helpsLoans and guarantees for new generation, storage and grid projects.
Watch forLong process; large project minimums.
Federal sites
How it helpsDOE picked Idaho National Lab, Oak Ridge, Paducah and Savannah River (July 2025) for private AI data center and energy projects.
Watch forSite-by-site solicitations; suited to large developers.
